The New Blood Pressure Guidelines: What They Mean for You

Oct 27, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 5 min read

For the first time since 2017, America's leading heart organizations have updated how high blood pressure should be diagnosed and treated. Here's what changed, and what it means the next time someone wraps a cuff around your arm.

Why This Update Matters

High blood pressure is one of the biggest drivers of heart attack, stroke, and kidney disease, and it affects nearly half of all American adults. It's also remarkably easy to miss. Fewer than 60% of people who have it know they do, and only about one in five have it under control. Most people feel completely fine right up until something goes wrong.

In 2025, the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology released their first major blood pressure guideline update in eight years. A new review in Current Hypertension Reports, coauthored by the guideline's lead author, breaks down what changed. Here's the plain English version.

What the Experts Looked At

A committee of 28 clinicians, working with 11 partner organizations, reviewed the research published since 2017 on how blood pressure should be measured, how heart risk should be estimated, and which treatments work best. Their goal: catch high blood pressure earlier, and match treatment more closely to each person's actual risk.

The number to know

The treatment goal for most adults is still under 130/80. But the new guideline goes further and encourages aiming for under 120/80 when that can be reached safely. Pregnancy and a few other situations are exceptions, so your own target is something to set with your doctor.

The Biggest Changes

Most of the core advice from 2017 still stands. These are the updates most likely to affect you:

  • A smarter risk calculator. Doctors now use a tool called PREVENT, which estimates your 10 year and 30 year risk of heart attack, stroke, and heart failure starting at age 30, and factors in your kidney function. If your risk is low, you may get 3 to 6 months to bring your numbers down with lifestyle changes before medication comes into the picture. If you have diabetes, kidney disease, or a history of heart disease, treatment starts sooner.
  • Two medications, one pill. For readings of 140/90 or higher, the guideline now recommends starting with two medications combined into a single pill. It brings blood pressure under control faster, and one pill a day is easier to stick with than two.
  • Home readings count more than ever. Home monitoring is now central to both diagnosis and ongoing treatment. It helps catch “white coat” readings that spike only in the clinic, and “masked” high blood pressure that looks normal in the clinic but runs high the rest of the day. Smartwatches and other cuffless devices aren't accurate enough yet and are not recommended for diagnosis.
  • A common hidden cause gets a closer look. Primary aldosteronism, a hormone condition of the adrenal glands, is behind 5 to 10% of all high blood pressure and up to 20% of hard to control cases. Yet fewer than 2% of people who should be tested ever are. Screening is now recommended for anyone with stage 2 or resistant high blood pressure.
  • Your brain benefits too. The guideline now firmly recommends keeping the top number under 130 to lower the risk of memory problems and dementia.

Updates for Specific Groups

  • Diabetes or kidney disease: Certain medication types (ACE inhibitors or ARBs) are now more strongly preferred because they protect the kidneys as well as the heart.
  • Pregnancy: High readings should be treated quickly, and some blood pressure medications are now on a longer list to avoid. If you have high blood pressure or are at risk for preeclampsia, your doctor may recommend low dose aspirin starting at 10 to 12 weeks.
  • Hard to control blood pressure: For people whose numbers stay high despite several medications, a procedure called renal denervation is now an option to discuss with a specialist. It adds to medication rather than replacing it.

A note on salt substitutes

The guideline now supports salt substitutes made with potassium for some patients. They aren't safe for everyone, though, especially people with serious kidney disease, so check with us before making the switch.

A Few Things Worth Keeping in Mind

  • A guideline is a starting point, not your prescription. Your right target depends on your age, health history, and overall risk.
  • Lower isn't automatically better for everyone. If you feel dizzy or lightheaded when you stand up after a medication change, let your doctor know. Never stop or adjust your medication on your own.
  • Some questions are still open. Researchers are still working out the best approach for younger adults whose bottom number alone runs high, and how new weight loss medications fit into the picture.

What You Can Actually Do

  • Know your numbers. If it's been more than a year since your last blood pressure check, schedule one.
  • Get a home monitor. Choose an upper arm cuff. They're easy to find at any Japanese electronics store or drugstore. Sit quietly for 5 minutes with your back supported, feet flat, and arm resting at heart level, then take two readings a minute apart. Bring your log to your next visit.
  • Watch the hidden salt. Soy sauce, miso, pickles, and ramen broth add up fast in Japan. Small swaps, like leaving the broth in the bowl, make a real difference.
  • Build the everyday habits. Regular movement, the DASH eating pattern (lots of vegetables, fruit, and whole grains), less alcohol, and stress management all lower blood pressure on their own.
  • Ask about testing for hidden causes. If you're on two or more medications and your numbers are still high, ask whether you should be screened for primary aldosteronism.

The bottom line: The 2025 guideline keeps the same basic target, but shifts toward treating your whole risk picture rather than a single reading. Home monitoring, earlier combination treatment, and better screening for hidden causes all point the same way: catch problems sooner and treat them smarter. Your best first step is simply knowing your numbers.
