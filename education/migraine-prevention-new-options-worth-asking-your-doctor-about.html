Migraine Prevention: New Options Worth Asking Your Doctor About

Nov 17, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 5 min read

If migraines are costing you several days every month, you may not have to just ride them out. A newer group of preventive medications is now recommended as a first choice, and your primary care doctor can usually start one without a long wait for a specialist.

Why This Matters

Migraine affects about 57 million people in the US. It is two to three times more common in women, who also tend to have longer attacks, more disability, and slower recovery. Men are less likely to seek care, often have less typical symptoms, and are more often misdiagnosed. They have also been left out of much of the research: roughly 80% of trial participants have been women.

Many people assume migraine prevention means waiting to see a neurologist. In the US, that wait can run three to nine months or longer, and finding an English-speaking specialist here in Japan can add another hurdle. The good news is that some of the most effective prevention options today are well within what your primary care doctor can offer.

What the Latest Guidance Says

In 2024, the American Headache Society updated its position: medications that target a protein called CGRP are now considered a first choice for preventing migraine in adults, not a last resort after everything else has failed.

CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) is a protein in the nervous system that widens blood vessels and plays a central role in setting off a migraine. Blocking it can mean fewer attacks to begin with, rather than just treating each one after it starts.

Count headache days, not headaches

Prevention is worth discussing if you have four or more headache days a month that slow you down at all, five or more migraine days a month even without much impact, or three or more migraine days a month that knock you out completely. One attack that lasts two days counts as two days, and every kind of headache counts toward the total, not just migraines.

The Newer Options and How They Compare

Two kinds of medication target CGRP. They work in slightly different ways, but the results are similar:

  • Injections every one to three months: Erenumab, fremanezumab, and galcanezumab are pens you use at home; eptinezumab is an IV infusion every three months. Across studies, 40% to more than 60% of people cut their migraine days roughly in half within three months. Side effects are usually mild, mostly redness or soreness where you inject (letting the refrigerated pen warm to room temperature helps), and erenumab causes constipation in up to 15% of people. A good fit if you would rather not take a pill every day.
  • Tablets: Rimegepant is a tablet that dissolves on the tongue, taken every other day for prevention. It can also be used to stop an attack, which helps if you already know it works for you or if nausea makes swallowing pills hard. Atogepant is one tablet a day. About half of people taking it saw migraine days drop by 50% or more within three months. Side effects are mostly mild stomach upset, each in fewer than 6% of people. A good fit if you would rather avoid needles.
  • The older standbys: Beta blockers, venlafaxine, and possibly amitriptyline are still reasonable choices. Topiramate has fallen out of favor because of its side effects.

Because the injections and tablets perform about equally well, the choice usually comes down to what your insurance covers and what fits your life best. One practical note: atogepant interacts with a number of other medications, so make sure your doctor sees your full medication and supplement list.

The catch: cost

None of these medications come in a generic version yet. In the US, manufacturer savings programs can bring the monthly cost way down, sometimes to almost nothing. Coverage and availability for patients living in Japan can work differently, so this is a good thing to sort out with us before you start. See how we handle insurance and billing.

If Medication Alone Isn't Enough

Medication works best alongside other tools. Acupuncture carries the strongest possible recommendation (grade A) from the American Academy of Family Physicians for preventing frequent migraine. Cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness, and relaxation training also help. So do the basics: staying hydrated, drinking less alcohol, keeping caffeine moderate, sleeping enough, and noticing triggers like harsh lighting, loud noise, or strong smells. Some people also find green light therapy soothing during an attack.

If everything within primary care has been tried, that is the time to see a neurologist. Specialists can offer Botox injections (about 31 small injections across the head and neck every three months), nerve blocks, and several wearable or handheld nerve stimulation devices. We can help coordinate a specialist referral when it's time.

A Few Things Worth Keeping in Mind

  • Prevention takes patience. The results in these studies were measured at about three months. A few rough weeks at the start doesn't mean a medication has failed.
  • “About half” means not everyone. These are strong options, not guarantees. If there is no real improvement after three to four months, there are still other paths to try.
  • Migraine in men is often missed. Because symptoms in men can look less typical, it's worth asking about migraine directly if you have frequent headaches, even if they don't match the textbook picture.
  • Some headaches need urgent care, not prevention. A sudden, severe “worst ever” headache, or a headache with weakness, confusion, trouble speaking, fever, or a stiff neck, needs immediate attention. In Japan, call 119.

What You Can Actually Do

  • Track your headache days for a month. Mark every day with any headache and note whether it slowed you down. A phone note or calendar works fine.
  • Book a visit with your primary care doctor. Bring your headache log and a list of every medication and supplement you take. You can book with us in person or by telehealth.
  • Ask about CGRP options by name. Tell your doctor whether you would prefer an injection every month or two or a tablet, and ask what your insurance covers.
  • Plan a three-month check-in. Keep tracking your headache days after you start so you and your doctor can see clearly whether it's working.

The bottom line: If headaches are taking four or more days a month from you, prevention is worth a conversation. Newer medications that target CGRP are now a recommended first choice, about half of people cut their migraine days in half within three months, and your primary care doctor can usually get you started without waiting months for a specialist.
