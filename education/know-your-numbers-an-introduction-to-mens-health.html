Know Your Numbers: An Introduction to Men's Health

Nov 25, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 3 min read

Most men only see a doctor when something feels wrong. The trouble is, many of the conditions that matter most don't feel like anything at all until they're well established. This issue kicks off a short series to help you get ahead of them.

Men's Health Series, Introduction

Five practical issues to close out the year.

Why Feeling Fine Isn't Enough

High blood pressure, high cholesterol, prediabetes, and some cancers tend to develop quietly. You can feel completely normal for years while the numbers drift in the wrong direction. By the time symptoms show up, the problem is usually harder to treat.

Living overseas can make this easier to put off. Between deployments, PCS moves, and navigating care in a new country, the annual checkup is often the first thing to slip. Over the next four weeks, this series will take one topic at a time and keep it simple: what to track, what to ask, and when to act.

The Four Numbers Worth Knowing

Think of your health like the dashboard of a car. You don't need to watch every gauge, but a few of them tell you a lot about where things are headed:

  • Blood pressure, the most common silent risk factor for heart attack and stroke
  • Cholesterol, including LDL, HDL, and triglycerides
  • Blood sugar, usually checked with an A1c test
  • Weight and waist size, since fat carried around the middle is more closely tied to heart and metabolic risk

The trend matters more than the snapshot

A single reading tells you where you are today. Several readings over time tell you where you're going. Keep your results in one place, especially if you've been seen at different clinics as you've moved around, so you and your doctor can spot changes early.

A Few Things Worth Keeping in Mind

  • An abnormal result isn't a reason to panic. Ask what it means for you, whether it's expected, whether anything could have affected it, and whether you should be retested. Questions turn a number into a plan.
  • Symptoms overlap. Fatigue, low mood, and weight changes have many possible causes. Avoid jumping to conclusions, or to treatments, based on symptoms alone.
  • Screening is personal. The right tests and timing depend on your age, family history, and overall health. There's no single checklist that fits every man.
  • More tests aren't always better. Large test panels can be useful, but results are only meaningful when reviewed alongside your history by someone who knows you.

What You Can Actually Do

  • Book an annual checkup. Treat it like routine maintenance. You don't wait for the engine to fail before changing the oil. Here are 7 questions to bring to your annual wellness visit.
  • Get your baseline labs. Blood pressure, a cholesterol panel, and an A1c are a strong start. Ask whether an Lp(a) test makes sense for you.
  • Learn your family history. Find out who had heart disease, diabetes, or cancer, and at what age. Write it down and bring it to your visit.
  • Start the prostate conversation. Most guidelines suggest discussing screening around age 50, and earlier (as young as 40 to 45) for Black men or men with a close relative who had prostate cancer.
  • Speak up about how you're really doing. Sleep, mood, energy, and stress are part of your health. Mention them, even if they seem minor.

The bottom line: Feeling fine and being healthy aren't always the same thing. Knowing your key numbers, understanding your family history, and keeping up with routine care give you the best chance to catch problems early, while they're still easy to manage.
