Why Perimenopause Disrupts Your Sleep, and What Actually Helps

Oct 6, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 5 min read

If you're in your 40s and suddenly wide awake at 3 a.m., sometimes soaked in sweat and sometimes just wired, you're not imagining it. I hear this from patients all the time. There are real reasons it happens, and the good news is that it's treatable.

Why I Wanted to Write About This

Perimenopause is the stretch of years leading up to your final period. It often starts in your 40s and can last several years. Trouble sleeping is one of the most common things women bring up with me during this time, and it's also one of the things that gets brushed off most often as “just stress” or “just getting older.”

I don't think it should be brushed off. Poor sleep follows you into the rest of your day. It affects your mood, your focus at work, and your patience at home. As you'll see below, it's also closely tied to anxiety, depression, and even heart health. Understanding why it happens is the first step toward fixing it.

What the Research Looked At

For this article I pulled together several kinds of research rather than a single study:

  • Long-term studies that followed women through midlife. These include the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN), which has followed a large and diverse group of American women since the 1990s, and the Massachusetts Women's Health Study.
  • Brain and hormone research on how estrogen and progesterone affect the parts of the brain that control sleep and wakefulness.
  • Treatment research, including a large review of hormone therapy trials and The Menopause Society's 2022 guidance on hormone therapy.
  • Sleep and mood research presented at The Menopause Society's 2024 meeting, plus a clinical review on depression during this life stage.

Night Sweats, Hormones, and Your Brain's Sleep Switch

Most women get hot flashes or night sweats during this transition, and SWAN found they're closely linked to waking up at night. They also tend to last much longer than many women are told, often seven years or more, and they can start well before your periods stop. SWAN also found that women with more frequent or longer-lasting hot flashes tend to have less favorable heart health markers. That's one more reason I don't want you to just tough it out.

Hot flashes aren't the whole story, though. Estrogen and progesterone help run the brain systems that keep you asleep or awake, including one called the orexin system, which acts like a “wake switch.” Lab research suggests estrogen affects how active that switch is. So the ups and downs of your hormones during perimenopause may push you toward lighter, more broken sleep, even on nights without a single hot flash.

What I most want you to take away

Sleep problems during perimenopause have real, physical causes. They aren't a personal failing, and you don't have to simply wait them out. That means there's something we can actually treat.

Poor Sleep and Mood Go Hand in Hand

Doctors think of perimenopause as a “window of vulnerability” for depression. Research reviewed by Dr. Claudio Soares shows women are roughly two to four times more likely to have depression symptoms during this transition than in the years before it. That's true even for women who have never been depressed before.

Sleep seems to be a big part of that connection. Research presented at The Menopause Society's 2024 meeting found that women with sleep problems in and after perimenopause were more likely to report depression and anxiety. And the Massachusetts Women's Health Study found that menopause itself didn't raise depression risk as much as a long transition full of symptoms did: the hot flashes and the broken sleep.

In other words, getting your sleep back on track may help protect your mood too.

Where Hormone Therapy Fits In

A large review of clinical trials found that hormone therapy improved sleep quality, mostly for women who were also having hot flashes or night sweats. That makes sense to me. If the night sweats calm down, you stop waking up from them.

The Menopause Society's 2022 guidance calls hormone therapy the most effective treatment for hot flashes and night sweats. For healthy women under 60, or within 10 years of their last period, it says the benefits generally outweigh the risks. It isn't right for everyone. Your history of breast cancer, blood clots, stroke, or heart disease matters a great deal. If hormone therapy isn't a good fit, or you'd simply rather not use it, there are non-hormonal options too, and I'm happy to walk through them with you.

A Few Things I Want You to Keep in Mind

  • Some of the brain research comes from animal studies. The work on estrogen and the orexin “wake switch” is promising, but animal results don't always carry over to people.
  • Sleep and mood feed each other. Poor sleep can worsen mood, and low mood can worsen sleep, so it's not always clear which one came first.
  • Not every sleep problem in midlife is hormonal. Sleep apnea, thyroid problems, medications, alcohol, and stress can all look similar. That's why I'd rather check than guess.

What You Can Actually Do

  • Keep a two-week sleep and symptom log. Jot down your bedtime, wake-ups, night sweats, any alcohol, and how your mood is the next day. Bring it to your visit. It helps us get to the answer faster.
  • Keep your bedroom cool and dry. Japan's humid summers are hard on night sweats. Run the AC or dehumidifier overnight, and choose light, breathable bedding and sleepwear.
  • Go easy on the evening glass of wine. Alcohol can trigger hot flashes and break up your sleep later in the night, even if it helps you fall asleep.
  • Wake up at the same time every day. Keeping a steady wake-up time, weekends included, is one of the simplest ways to steady your sleep.
  • Be careful with over-the-counter sleep aids. Many of the ones sold at drugstores, here and back home, are antihistamines. They can leave you groggy and aren't meant for long-term use. Let's talk about options that treat the actual cause.
  • Tell me about your mood, not just your sleep. If you've felt low, anxious, or irritable for more than two weeks, please let me know. If you ever have thoughts of hurting yourself, reach out for help right away.
  • Ask about all your options. That includes hormone therapy, non-hormonal medications, and cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), a proven approach that doesn't involve medication.

The bottom line: Sleep problems during perimenopause are common, have real physical causes, and are closely tied to your mood and long-term health. They're also treatable. If you're not sleeping well, please don't wait it out. Let's talk about it.
