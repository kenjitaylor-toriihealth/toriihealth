Know Your Numbers: The Men's Health Questions Worth Asking

Nov 17, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 5 min read

Most men only see a doctor when something feels wrong. The trouble is, many of the conditions that matter most don't feel like anything at all until they're well established. Here's where to start.

Men's Health Series, Part 1

The questions every man should be asking his doctor: what to track, what to ask, and when to act.

Why Feeling Fine Isn't Enough

High blood pressure, high cholesterol, prediabetes, and some cancers tend to develop quietly. You can feel completely normal for years while the numbers drift in the wrong direction. By the time symptoms show up, the problem is usually harder to treat.

Living overseas can make this easier to put off. Between deployments, PCS moves, and navigating care in a new country, the annual checkup is often the first thing to slip. This series is meant to make it simple: what to track, what to ask, and when to act.

The Four Numbers Worth Knowing

Think of your health like the dashboard of a car. You don't need to watch every gauge, but a few of them tell you a lot about where things are headed:

  • Blood pressure, the most common silent risk factor for heart attack and stroke
  • Cholesterol, including LDL, HDL, and triglycerides
  • Blood sugar, usually checked with an A1c test
  • Weight and waist size, since fat carried around the middle is more closely tied to heart and metabolic risk

The trend matters more than the snapshot

A single reading tells you where you are today. Several readings over time tell you where you're going. Keep your results in one place, especially if you've been seen at different clinics as you've moved around, so you and your doctor can spot changes early.

Three Risks to Understand Early

These are the questions we hear most often from men in our practice, and the ones most worth bringing to your next visit:

  • Diabetes. Prediabetes rarely causes symptoms. Your risk is higher if you're 45 or older, carry extra weight around your waist, have a parent or sibling with Type 2 diabetes, aren't very active, or already have high blood pressure or cholesterol. An A1c blood test shows your average blood sugar over the past two to three months. Prediabetes is a warning sign, not a life sentence, and it often responds well to change.
  • Heart disease and family history. If a parent or sibling had heart disease at a young age, your own risk may be higher. One inherited factor worth knowing is lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a). Many experts now suggest checking it at least once, particularly if heart disease runs in your family. You can't change your genes, but you can manage blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, smoking, and activity.
  • Prostate health. This doesn't always mean screening right away, but it does mean starting the conversation. Most guidelines suggest discussing it around age 50, and earlier (as young as 40 to 45) for Black men, men with a father or brother who had prostate cancer, or men with a strong family history of breast or ovarian cancer.

If you don't know your family health history, now is a good time to ask. A few phone calls home can make a real difference in how we plan your care.

What about testosterone?

Testosterone does drop gradually with age, though usually less dramatically than people expect. Low energy, lower sex drive, mood changes, and muscle loss can point to low testosterone, but they're just as often linked to poor sleep, stress, weight gain, or depression. If something feels different, testing can help, but it works best as part of a full evaluation rather than a quick answer.

Mental Health Is Health

Men are less likely to ask for help when they're struggling, and depression doesn't always look like sadness. It can show up as irritability or anger, pulling away from family and friends, changes in sleep, or losing interest in things you used to enjoy. Time away from home and the usual support network can make all of this harder to notice and harder to talk about.

Asking for help is a sign of strength, and a conversation with your doctor, a friend, or your partner is a good first step. We also offer behavioral health care in English, in person or by telehealth.

A Few Things Worth Keeping in Mind

  • An abnormal result isn't a reason to panic. Ask what it means for you, whether it's expected, whether anything could have affected it, and whether you should be retested. Questions turn a number into a plan.
  • Symptoms overlap. Fatigue, low mood, and weight changes have many possible causes. Avoid jumping to conclusions, or to treatments, based on symptoms alone.
  • Screening is personal. The right tests and timing depend on your age, family history, and overall health. There's no single checklist that fits every man.
  • More tests aren't always better. Large test panels can be useful, but results are only meaningful when reviewed alongside your history by someone who knows you.

What You Can Actually Do

  • Book an annual checkup. Treat it like routine maintenance. You don't wait for the engine to fail before changing the oil. Here are 7 questions to bring to your annual wellness visit.
  • Get your baseline labs. Blood pressure, a cholesterol panel, and an A1c are a strong start. Ask whether an Lp(a) test makes sense for you.
  • Learn your family history. Find out who had heart disease, diabetes, or cancer, and at what age. Write it down and bring it to your visit.
  • Speak up about how you're really doing. Sleep, mood, energy, and stress are part of your health. Mention them, even if they seem minor.

The bottom line: Feeling fine and being healthy aren't always the same thing. Knowing your key numbers, understanding your family history, and keeping up with routine care give you the best chance to catch problems early, while they're still easy to manage.
