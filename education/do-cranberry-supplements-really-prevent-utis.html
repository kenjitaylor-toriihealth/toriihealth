Do Cranberry Supplements Really Prevent UTIs? What 50 Studies Show

Oct 27, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 4 min read

Cranberry for bladder health sounds like an old wives' tale. But a 2023 Cochrane review of 50 randomized controlled trials, covering more than 8,800 people, found it really does lower the risk of urinary tract infections for some groups. Here's what the research shows, who benefits, and how to pick a product that actually works.

Why This Question Matters

Urinary tract infections are among the most common reasons people see a doctor, and for many women they keep coming back. Each infection usually means another round of antibiotics, and repeated antibiotic use raises the risk of side effects and resistant bacteria. So a simple, low risk way to prevent UTIs is worth a close look.

Cranberries contain compounds called proanthocyanidins (PACs). PACs make it harder for E. coli, the bacteria behind most UTIs, to stick to the lining of the bladder. If the bacteria can't hold on, they're more likely to be flushed out before they cause an infection.

What the Research Looked At

In April 2023, researchers led by Gabrielle Williams published an updated review titled Cranberries for Preventing Urinary Tract Infections in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. Cochrane is an independent international network known for some of the most rigorous evidence reviews in medicine, and its authors reported no financial ties to cranberry makers. This review was first published in 1998 and has been updated five times; this version added 26 new studies.

In total, the researchers pooled 50 randomized controlled trials with 8,857 participants, the type of study design considered the gold standard for testing whether a treatment works. They compared cranberry juice, tablets, and capsules against placebo, no treatment, antibiotics, and probiotics. Two reviewers independently assessed every study for quality and bias, and the main outcome they counted was UTIs that caused symptoms and were confirmed by a urine culture.

Who Benefits, and Who Doesn't

Across 26 trials and more than 6,200 participants, cranberry products lowered the risk of a confirmed UTI by about 30%. The researchers rated this as moderate certainty evidence, meaning the true effect is likely close to this estimate. The benefit was clearest in three groups:

  • Women with recurrent UTIs: about a 26% lower risk (8 trials, 1,555 participants)
  • Children: roughly cut in half (5 trials, 504 participants)
  • People at risk because of a medical procedure (such as surgery, radiation, or catheter use): also roughly cut in half (6 trials, 1,434 participants)

On the other hand, the review found little or no benefit for older adults living in care facilities, for pregnant women, or for people whose bladder doesn't empty fully because of a nerve or muscle condition. Across 10 trials, stomach upset was about as common with cranberry as with placebo.

The one to weigh most heavily

In two trials with 385 participants, cranberry worked about as well as daily preventive antibiotics. The evidence there is limited, but for women who get repeat infections, cranberry may offer a way to cut down on antibiotic use. Talk with your doctor before swapping one for the other.

A Few Things Worth Keeping in Mind

  • Cranberry prevents; it doesn't treat. If you have burning, urgency, fever, or back pain, you need to be seen. Cranberry won't clear an active infection.
  • Not every product is the same. Studies used different juices, tablets, and doses, and the review couldn't tell whether juice or tablets work better.
  • Juice can carry a lot of sugar. Many cranberry drinks are mostly sweetened juice with little actual cranberry, which is one reason a supplement may be the more practical choice.
  • Supplements aren't tightly regulated. What's on the label isn't always what's in the bottle, so quality checks matter.
  • Check for interactions. If you take a blood thinner such as warfarin, ask your doctor before starting cranberry.

What You Can Actually Do

  • Look for at least 36 mg of PACs. Choose a cranberry supplement that lists at least 36 mg of proanthocyanidins per daily dose.
  • Take it every day. Cranberry works as daily prevention, not something to start once symptoms appear.
  • Choose a tested product. Look for the USP Verified mark or another sign of independent third party testing. A quick online search will turn up products that meet both criteria.
  • Ask about it if UTIs keep coming back. If you've had two or more infections in six months, let's talk about whether cranberry, antibiotics, or another approach fits you best.

The bottom line: A large review of 50 studies found that cranberry products can lower the risk of UTIs for women with recurrent infections, children, and people at risk after a medical procedure, though not for everyone. If it's a fit for you, a daily, independently tested supplement with at least 36 mg of PACs is the way to go.
