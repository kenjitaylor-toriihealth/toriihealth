Why Your Body Aches With a Cold or Flu

Oct 7, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 5 min read

Sore muscles, a pounding head, that heavy run-down feeling. When a cold or flu hits, it can feel like your whole body is under attack, not just your nose and throat. Here's what's actually going on, and what helps.

Why Those Aches Matter

Viral upper respiratory infections (URIs), the colds and flu-like bugs that circulate every fall and winter, are among the most common reasons people see a doctor. The average adult catches 2 to 4 colds a year, and school-age kids can catch 6 to 10. With flu season getting started here in Japan, many of our families are about to get reacquainted with the full-body misery that comes along with them.

Most of us assume the virus itself is what makes us feel so terrible. The research tells a more interesting story, and understanding it can help you make smarter choices about what to take, when to rest, and when to call us.

What the Research Looked At

A review by a team of Polish researchers (Kuchar and colleagues), published in Advances in Experimental Medicine and Biology, pulled together findings from 129 studies on why viral respiratory infections cause the symptoms they do. Rather than focusing on a single virus, they looked at what the four most common culprits (rhinovirus, coronavirus, RSV, and influenza) have in common.

They covered the whole symptom list: sneezing, runny nose, sore throat, cough, fever, headache, fatigue, and the muscle aches that leave so many of us stuck on the couch.

The headline finding

It's mostly your immune system, not the virus. Very different viruses produce nearly identical symptoms in nearly the same order. That's why a doctor usually can't tell which virus you have from symptoms alone: what you're feeling is your body's own defense response.

Why Your Muscles Ache (and Why You Feel So Lousy)

The infection may start in your nose and throat, but the immune response travels everywhere. Here's how a local infection turns into a full-body experience:

  • Cytokines carry the message. Immune cells release signaling proteins called cytokines (such as TNF-alpha and interleukins) to coordinate the fight. They circulate throughout your body, which is how a stuffy nose ends up with aching legs.
  • Prostaglandin E2 turns up the pain. Cytokines trigger production of prostaglandin E2, a chemical that heightens pain signals and raises your brain's temperature set point. The same pathway drives both fever and muscle aches.
  • Muscle protein gets broken down. TNF-alpha has been linked to the breakdown of muscle proteins, part of why you feel weak as well as sore.
  • Fatigue and low mood are real effects. Cytokines act directly on the brain, contributing to exhaustion, irritability, trouble concentrating, and loss of appetite. People treated with interferon, an immune-signaling medication, report the same flu-like malaise.

By the numbers

About half of people with a common cold report muscle aches. In one clinical trial, roughly 60% of patients with a cold and sore throat also had a headache, and about 1 in 3 experienced chills.

Why the Flu Hits Harder Than a Cold

Common cold viruses like rhinovirus cause very little actual damage to the cells lining your airways. Influenza is different: it damages those cells and sets off a much stronger cytokine response, and the intensity of flu symptoms tracks closely with cytokine levels. That's why the flu so often comes with high fever, cough, and aches that feel like you were hit by a truck.

Age matters too. Adults have met many cold viruses before, so a typical cold rarely causes fever. Infants and young children are seeing these viruses for the first time, mount a stronger immune response, and run fevers much more often.

One myth worth retiring

Green or yellow mucus does not mean you need antibiotics. The color comes from enzymes inside your own immune cells and reflects how much inflammation is going on, not whether bacteria are involved.

A Few Things Worth Keeping in Mind

  • This is a review, not a new trial. It summarizes existing research published through 2014, and some of the mechanisms come from lab studies rather than studies in people.
  • Feeling better isn't the same as getting better faster. Pain relievers ease aches and fever, but it's still unclear whether treating symptoms shortens the illness itself.
  • Some popular advice hasn't been proven. The researchers note that common tips like steam inhalation haven't been backed by solid evidence. They may feel soothing, but they aren't a cure.
  • Everyone's immune response is different. Age, health conditions, and prior exposure all shape how sick a given virus makes you.

What You Can Actually Do

  • Target the aches directly. Acetaminophen or ibuprofen work on the same pain and fever pathway described above. Follow label dosing, and check with us first if you have kidney disease, stomach ulcers, are pregnant, or take a blood thinner.
  • Never give aspirin to children or teens with a viral illness. It's linked to Reye syndrome, a rare but serious condition affecting the liver and brain.
  • Read Japanese cold medicine labels carefully. Many all-in-one cold remedies sold at local drugstores already contain acetaminophen (アセトアミノフェン) or ibuprofen (イブプロフェン). Adding a separate pain reliever on top is an easy way to take too much. Snap a photo of the box and send it to us on WhatsApp if you're unsure.
  • Give yourself permission to rest. That drained feeling is part of your immune response doing its work. Skip hard workouts while you're feverish or achy.
  • Know when it's more than a cold. Sudden high fever with severe body aches may be the flu, and flu antivirals work best when started within about 48 hours, so call early. Also reach out if symptoms last beyond 10 days, improve and then get worse, or come with trouble breathing, chest pain, or a seizure with fever in a young child.
  • Get your flu shot. Fall is the ideal time, and it's the best way to avoid the worst version of these aches altogether. Book a visit to get yours.

The bottom line: The body aches that come with a viral URI are a sign your immune system is hard at work, driven by cytokines and prostaglandins rather than direct damage from the virus. The right pain reliever and some rest can make you far more comfortable, and knowing the warning signs of flu helps you get treatment when it matters most.
