PCOS Has a New Name: What PMOS Means for Your Care

Oct 20, 2026 · Written by Dr. Kenji Taylor, MD · 4 min read

If you've been diagnosed with polycystic ovary syndrome, you'll soon start hearing a different name. A global group of experts and patients has renamed it polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, or PMOS. Here's why, and what does and doesn't change for you.

Why a Name Change Matters

PCOS affects about one in eight women, making it one of the most common hormone conditions there is. Yet many women spend years getting bounced between specialists before anyone connects the dots, and some never get a clear diagnosis at all.

Experts now say part of the problem is the name itself. “Polycystic ovary syndrome” suggests the condition is about cysts on the ovaries. It isn't. The small “cysts” seen on ultrasound are actually immature egg follicles, not harmful cysts, and plenty of people with the condition don't have them at all. Meanwhile, the old name says nothing about the hormone and metabolism problems that drive much of the long-term health risk.

How the New Name Was Chosen

This wasn't a decision made by a small committee. The process, published in The Lancet in 2026 and led by Helena Teede and an international team of researchers, brought together 56 leading academic, clinical, and patient organizations from around the world.

Over several rounds of global surveys, 14,360 people, including women living with PCOS and health professionals from every world region, weighed in. Structured expert workshops then narrowed down the options, and the team also studied how a new name could realistically be rolled out across health systems.

The headline finding

Participants put scientific accuracy, clarity, and avoiding stigma at the top of the list. They chose a truly accurate new name over keeping the familiar PCOS acronym or switching to something vague. The result: polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, or PMOS.

What Each Word in PMOS Tells You

The new name is built to describe what the condition actually does in the body:

  • Polyendocrine: it involves several hormone systems, not just the ovaries. Higher levels of androgens (hormones like testosterone) and insulin resistance are common.
  • Metabolic: it affects how the body handles sugar and fat, raising the risk of type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol, and other heart health risk factors, even in women who aren't overweight.
  • Ovarian: it still affects ovulation, which shows up as irregular or missed periods and can make it harder to get pregnant.

Just as important is what was left out: the word “cysts.” The researchers argue the old name contributed to delayed diagnosis, fragmented care, and stigma, and even held back research and health policy. A name that reflects the whole condition should make it easier for doctors and patients alike to look beyond the ovaries.

A Few Things Worth Keeping in Mind

  • The name changed. The condition didn't. If you were diagnosed with PCOS, you have PMOS. How it's diagnosed and treated stays the same.
  • You'll see both names for a while. The rollout is planned as a gradual transition, with education and updates to health systems and disease classification still under way. Your records, lab orders, and insurance claims may say PCOS for some time.
  • This was a naming consensus, not a clinical trial. It doesn't bring new treatments with it. What it changes is how the condition is understood and talked about.

What You Can Actually Do

  • If you've been diagnosed, keep your current treatment plan. Nothing about your care needs to change because of the new name.
  • Use the name change as a reminder to check your metabolic health. If it's been a while, ask about blood sugar, cholesterol, and blood pressure checks.
  • Don't overlook your mood. Anxiety and depression are more common with this condition, and they deserve attention too.
  • If you have symptoms but no diagnosis, bring them up. Irregular periods, acne, unwanted hair growth, trouble managing weight, or difficulty getting pregnant are all worth a conversation.
  • If you see “PMOS” on a form or in an article, don't worry. It's the same condition you already know as PCOS.

The bottom line: PCOS is now called polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, or PMOS, a name chosen through a global process to reflect that this is a whole-body hormone and metabolic condition, not a problem with cysts. Your diagnosis and treatment stay the same, but the new name is a good reason to make sure your care covers your hormones, metabolism, and mental health, not just your cycle.
